The percentage increase formula is **(new value − old value) ÷ old value × 100**. A negative answer means a decrease. The old value, where you started, always goes underneath.

## Percentage increase, step by step

A price rises from 80 to 100.

1. Subtract the old value from the new one: 100 − 80 = 20.
2. Divide by the old value: 20 ÷ 80 = 0.25.
3. Multiply by 100: **25% increase**.

## Percentage decrease

The price then falls from 100 back to 80.

1. 80 − 100 = −20.
2. −20 ÷ 100 = −0.2.
3. That is a **20% decrease**.

The two moves are the same size in money but different percentages, because they start from different values. This is why a 25% rise is undone by a 20% fall, not a 25% fall.

## Apply a percentage, or reverse one

| You want | Formula | Example |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Value after an increase of p% | old × (1 + p ÷ 100) | 80 plus 25% = 100 |
| Value after a decrease of p% | old × (1 − p ÷ 100) | 100 minus 20% = 80 |
| Original before an increase of p% | new ÷ (1 + p ÷ 100) | 100 before a 25% rise = 80 |

To reverse a change, divide. Subtracting the same percentage from the new value gives the wrong answer.

## Stacked changes

Percentages that happen one after another do not add. A 10% rise followed by another 10% rise is 1.10 × 1.10 = 1.21, or **21%**. The same applies to discounts: the [discount calculator](/discount-calculator/) shows the real total of stacked offers.

## Percentage points are different

If a rate moves from 4% to 5%, it rose by **1 percentage point**. As a percentage change, (5 − 4) ÷ 4 × 100 = **25%**. Say which one you mean, especially for interest rates and survey results.

## Change or difference?

Use percentage **change** when one value came first. When comparing two values with no obvious starting point, such as two quotes, **percentage difference** divides by their average instead. The [Percentage Calculator](/percentage-calculator/) has both and shows the formula it used.
